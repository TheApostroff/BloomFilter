The Great Gatsby

"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."

In my younger and more vulnerable years, my father gave me advice that I've been turning over in my mind ever since. "Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone," he told me, "just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had."

He didn't say any more, but we've always been unusually communicative in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more than that. In consequence, I'm inclined to reserve all judgments, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores.

The Fitzgerald prose shimmers on the page, describing the Jazz Age with unmatched elegance and precision. The narrative voice of Nick Carraway provides a perfect window into the excess and moral decay of the era.

"All right," said Daisy and an expression of childlike wonder appeared again in her face. "These things excite me so," she whispered. "If that's what you want."

"That's exactly what I want," I confessed with a gasp quite as passionate as hers had been.

The green light across the bay symbolizes Gatsby's dream, forever out of reach yet eternally pursued. The novel captures the essence of American ambition and the futility of chasing an impossible past.

"Can't repeat the past?" "Why of course you can!" "I hope so," said Gatsby doubtfully, "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us."

This masterpiece of American literature remains as relevant today as it was when first published in 1925.
